There was a running joke about Hilary Putnam. Every decade, a new realism. Colleagues kept count of the conversions; students joked that the reading list depended on which Putnam you got; one quip had him as the philosopher who refuted himself more thoroughly than his critics ever managed. Scientific realism in the sixties. Internal realism from the late seventies. Natural realism in the nineties. Three positions, one career, and the word "realism" stretched across all of them like a tent over a man who kept moving.

The three are quickly felt, if not quickly argued. Scientific realism says: our best theories are approximately true, and the things they posit — electrons, genes, fields — really exist; anything less would make the success of science a miracle. Internal realism says: true, but only within a conceptual scheme; what counts as an object depends on the framework in use, and a world of three individuals holds three objects or seven depending on how you count — with no answer beneath the counting. Natural realism says: enough apparatus — we perceive the world directly, mind and body and world one threefold cord, and the ordinary claim that the cup is on the table needs no theory standing behind it. Notice what changes each time: not just the doctrine, but the *register*. You can hear it. The first speaks laboratory. The second speaks framework. The third speaks kitchen table.

And that is the turn worth making. Read the three not as three answers to one question — *what is real?* — but as three languages in which the word "real" does its work. In the language of science, "real" earns its keep exactly as scientific realism says: electrons are as real as anything the vocabulary can name, and the no-miracles argument is that language taking its own success seriously. In the language of frameworks, "real" works scheme-internally, precisely as internal realism describes — three objects here, seven there, both in order. In the language of the everyday, "real" needs no argument at all: the cup is on the table, and only a philosopher would ask for more. Each realism is right — in its language. What none of them gets to be is the owner of the word.

Read that way, the career stops looking like a series of retreats. Putnam didn't refute himself three times; he gave up, three times, a vocabulary's claim to be *the* vocabulary. The scientific realist who stopped claiming that science's language was the one true description. The internal realist who stopped claiming that the scheme's-eye view was the last word about truth. What the joke counted as conversions were renunciations — each one the same renunciation, made against a different backdrop. The man kept changing his mind the way a walker keeps changing his footing: the ground moved; the direction didn't.

One aside, because it is too good to leave out. The philosopher Putnam fought longest and hardest — thirty years of papers and rejoinders — was Richard Rorty, who held that there are only vocabularies, all the way down, none of them answerable to anything deeper. And the position Putnam finally rested in sits nearer to that than to the scientific realism he started from — as I read it, anyway; Putnam would have objected, and did, to the end. There is something fitting in it: a man who spent his career giving up vocabularies' claims to own the word, still fighting the man who said that's all there ever was to give up.
