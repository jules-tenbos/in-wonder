# David Chalmers (1966–)

Chalmers named the problem his field now orbits. "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" (1995) drew the line: the *easy* problems — explaining discrimination, integration, report, attention, the functions — are hard in the ordinary way, tractable by cognitive science; the **hard problem** is why any of that functioning is *accompanied by experience* at all. Why is there something it is like? Every functional explanation, he argued, leaves the further question open — explain the performance, and the accompaniment remains unexplained. The naming stuck because it packaged a century of unease into a usable distinction, and the hard problem became the organising challenge of the field of consciousness studies — the journals, associations, conferences, and adversarial collaborations that had begun assembling in 1994, the year before the paper. The hard problem is distinct from the emergence question, though the two are frequently run together: whether experience arises from physical organisation is one issue; why any arising is *accompanied by* experience is his.

## Life

Born 20 April 1966 in Sydney; raised in Sydney and Adelaide; a bronze medallist at the 1982 International Mathematical Olympiad, he studied mathematics at Adelaide (1983–86) and began at Oxford before turning to consciousness. PhD at Indiana (1993) — in philosophy and cognitive science, at Douglas [Hofstadter](/positioning/persons/h/hofstadter/)'s Center for Research on Concepts and Cognition — an origin among the modellers, not the metaphysicians, which colours the corpus: technical facility and sympathy for computation run through even the anti-materialist arguments. A postdoctoral fellowship at Washington University (1993–95) began the collaboration with Andy Clark; after Santa Cruz, he directed Arizona's Center for Consciousness Studies (1999–2004) — the Tucson conferences, begun by Stuart Hameroff in 1994, predate him, and "Facing Up" was first a talk there — and after years at ANU he is professor of philosophy and neural science at NYU and co-director of its Center for Mind, Brain and Consciousness. Co-founder of the Association for the Scientific Study of Consciousness — at whose 2023 meeting his famous 25-year wager with the neuroscientist Christof Koch (a case of wine on whether a clear neural signature of consciousness would be found by 2023) was settled, publicly and amicably, in the philosopher's favour.

## The programme

**The hard problem and the conceivability argument.** *The Conscious Mind* (1996) gives the challenge its full machinery. The central argument: zombies — beings physically and functionally identical to us with no experience — are coherently conceivable; what is conceivable in the ideal limit is metaphysically possible; so experience does not logically supervene on the physical; so materialism is false. The supporting analysis holds that physical vocabulary specifies structure and dynamics, and structure entails only structure — experience, as the intrinsic term left over, cannot be reached from it. The apparatus was built with unusual care (the two-dimensional semantic framework, developed partly for this purpose, is his major technical contribution to philosophy of language in its own right), which is why the argument has been attacked at every joint for thirty years without being retired.

**Naturalistic dualism.** Chalmers's positive picture: experience as a fundamental feature, alongside mass and charge, connected to the physical by basic *psychophysical laws* — science extended rather than abandoned. He has explored panpsychism and Russellian monism as candidate shapes for those laws, holding the options open rather than settling — and engaging their own hardest difficulty in print: the combination problem, how micro-experiences could compose a unified mind, which he treats as panpsychism's version of the hard problem rather than a reason to dismiss it. Strong emergence, in his taxonomy ("Strong and Weak Emergence," 2006), has exactly one serious instance: consciousness — everything else called emergent he classes as weak. And he has answered the standard historical objection directly: the analogy with chemistry and vitalism does not apply, in his terms, because the properties the emergentists staked were functional — characterised by what they do, hence derivable in principle once the mechanisms were known — while experience is not a functional property, so its underivability is not of the kind that better theory has repeatedly dissolved. The claim is not "we cannot yet derive it" but "no derivation of functions could suffice."

**The extended mind.** With Andy Clark (1998): cognition is not skull-bound — when external props (the notebook, the phone) play the functional role inner memory plays, they are part of the cognitive process. The paper became one of philosophy's most-cited and seeded the 4E literature. The extended-mind Chalmers is a thoroughgoing functionalist about *cognition*; the hard-problem Chalmers denies functionalism reaches *experience* — the two stances divide precisely along his easy/hard line.

**The later reach.** "The Meta-Problem of Consciousness" (2018) turns the method on itself: explain why we *judge* there is a hard problem — a tractable, functional question whose answer might either dissolve or vindicate the hard problem proper. *Reality+* (2022) argues virtual worlds are genuine realities and simulation hypotheses are not sceptical defeaters — techno-philosophy continuous with the Hofstadter formation. The singularity paper (2010) helped make superintelligence a respectable philosophical topic.

**The live disputes.** Every element is contested. Type-B materialists grant the conceivability and deny the possibility (the identity known a posteriori); Dennett rejects the data themselves — the "hard problem" as an artifact of inflated first-person authority, qualia quined; illusionism (Frankish) develops that line into a position — the phenomenal is an introspective illusion, and explaining the illusion replaces the hard problem; the phenomenal-concept strategy locates the gap in our concepts of experience rather than in the world; Stoljar's ignorance hypothesis holds we lack the relevant physical concepts; the quality-space structuralists press that experience is relational structure as soon as it is described. Chalmers has engaged all of these in print, conceding none, and curating the debate as much as fighting it — his anthologies and surveys are the field's standard maps, including of his opponents.

## Where Chalmers stops

The programme names the problem and defends its reality; it does not solve it, and Chalmers says so — the psychophysical laws are a promissory structure, their form unknown, and *The Conscious Mind* calls itself a search for a theory rather than the theory. The conceivability engine's own limit he has acknowledged in the meta-problem turn: if the judgment that there is a hard problem has a full functional explanation, the argument's data are themselves up for reassessment — a door he opened deliberately. And the position's price is stated in his own accounting: fundamental psychophysical laws keyed to complex configurations, and either a failure of physical causal closure or the near-epiphenomenalism of experience — costs he has preferred to the alternatives, while agreeing they are costs.

## Key works

- "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness," *Journal of Consciousness Studies* (1995) — the hard problem named
- *The Conscious Mind* (Oxford, 1996) — the full statement: zombies, structure and dynamics, naturalistic dualism
- Clark & Chalmers, "The Extended Mind," *Analysis* (1998) — cognition past the skull
- "Strong and Weak Emergence" (2006) — the taxonomy the emergence debate uses
- "The Meta-Problem of Consciousness," *Journal of Consciousness Studies* (2018) — the method turned on itself
- *Reality+* (Norton, 2022) — virtual worlds as genuine realities
