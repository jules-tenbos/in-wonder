# Mark Bedau (1953–)

Bedau gave the sciences of complexity their working definition of emergence. Weak emergence, in his 1997 formulation: a macro-state of a system is weakly emergent when it can be derived from the system's micro-dynamics and external conditions — *but only by simulation*. No closed-form shortcut, no analytical summary; to know what the system does, crawl through what it does. The definition threads a needle the older debate had left impassable: the emergent is genuinely derivable (no further facts, no violation of physics) yet genuinely autonomous in practice (the derivation is incompressible — the simulation *is* the shortest path). Conway's Game of Life, traffic jams, flocking, market crashes: the paradigm cases of the complexity sciences are exactly of this kind, and Bedau's account says why they feel emergent without magic. His contrast classes complete the map: nominal emergence (any macro-property no micro-part can have — ubiquitous, innocuous) and strong emergence (irreducible causal powers — which he argues science has no use for: "uncomfortably like magic").

---

## Life

Born 1953. BA in philosophy at Reed College (1977); PhD at Berkeley (1985). He returned to Reed, where he has taught philosophy and humanities since, running in parallel a career inside the science he philosophises about: long-time editor-in-chief of the MIT Press journal *Artificial Life*, co-organiser of the field's conferences, co-founder of the European Center for Living Technology in Venice, and a participant in protocell research — the attempt to build minimal chemical systems exhibiting life's signature capacities. The dual position — philosopher of a field, working inside it — shapes the corpus: his philosophy is answerable to models he can run.

## The programme

**Weak emergence.** The 1997 statement ("Weak Emergence," *Philosophical Perspectives*) and its later development ("Downward Causation and Autonomy in Weak Emergence," 2002) define the position. The criterion is explanatory incompressibility: the macro is fixed by the micro, and *deriving* it requires walking the micro-steps. Underivability-in-practice replaces underivability-in-principle — which relocates emergence from ontology to the structure of derivation, deliberately. Downward causation gets a deflated rescue — this is Bedau's own reading of it, one of the two the field runs: macro-patterns constrain their components in the way a traffic jam constrains the cars that constitute it — real, explanatory, and nothing over and above the aggregated micro-causation.

**The philosophy of artificial life.** Bedau's second contribution is treating ALife as philosophy's laboratory: cellular automata and evolving digital organisms as thought experiments that actually run. His long-standing empirical measures of evolutionary activity — statistics designed to detect whether a system exhibits ongoing, open-ended adaptive novelty — gave the field its standing test, and its standing embarrassment: by those measures, no artificial system yet matches the biosphere's open-endedness. He made the honest negative result famous, and "open-ended evolution" a named grand challenge.

**Life as a cluster, aliveness as graded.** On the definitional question ALife forces — what is life? — Bedau has argued for treating life as a cluster of capacities centred on supple adaptation, with borderline cases expected rather than embarrassing. The protocell work is the constructive face of the same stance: if aliveness is graded, building upward from chemistry is a research programme, not a category mistake — with the ethical and social questions of "living technology" taken as part of the work.

**The live disputes.** Weak emergence's standing objection is that it is epistemic only: Jessica Wilson and others press that incompressibility is a fact about derivation and explanation, not about the world — so weak emergence cannot ground the metaphysical autonomy its friends want from it. Bedau's reply is that the objection mistakes the ambition: underivability-without-simulation *is* an objective structural fact about a system's dynamics, and it is the fact the sciences actually use. Whether that is enough — whether "emergence" should name anything less than ontological novelty — is the live fault line between his camp and the metaphysicians', and it maps onto the field's deepest axis (epistemic versus ontological readings) rather than being resolvable inside either.

---

## Where Bedau stops

Weak emergence is a thesis about derivability, and Bedau keeps it there: it does not explain why certain macro-patterns are *robust* — why the same jam, flock, or crash re-forms across wildly different micro-configurations — a question he acknowledges and leaves to the sciences of universality. The strong-emergence question is closed by his lights rather than answered: the position rules irreducible causal powers scientifically idle by argument, and consciousness — the one case where many insist something more is at stake — he sets aside rather than treats. And the open-endedness of evolution, by his own measures, remains unachieved in any artificial system — the programme's standing boundary, recorded in his own results.

## Key works

- "Weak Emergence," *Philosophical Perspectives* 11 (1997) — the definition
- "Downward Causation and Autonomy in Weak Emergence," *Principia* (2002) — the deflated downward causation
- Bedau & Humphreys (eds.), *Emergence: Contemporary Readings in Philosophy and Science* (MIT, 2008) — the field's standard anthology
- "The scientific and philosophical scope of artificial life," *Artificial Life* (2003) — the ALife programme stated
- Rasmussen, Bedau et al. (eds.), *Protocells: Bridging Nonliving and Living Matter* (MIT, 2009) — the constructive face
