# Émile Durkheim (1858–1917)

Durkheim founded sociology on a single claim: that society is a reality *sui generis* — of its own kind, irreducible to the psychology or biology of its members. Social facts — ways of acting, thinking, and feeling that are general across a society, exterior to any individual, and *constraining* on each — are, he insisted, things: to be studied as things, explained by other social facts, never explained away downward. When individual consciousnesses associate, something forms that none of them contains — as he put it, the hardness of bronze is in none of the copper, tin, or lead. The claim made a science possible: a domain of its own facts needs its own discipline. It is also the social sciences' founding irreducibility claim — the contemporary emergence literature reads Durkheim as its earliest sustained practitioner (Sawyer applies the label explicitly), though the word was never his.

---

## Life

Born 15 April 1858 in Épinal, Lorraine, into a family of rabbis — a lineage he left, without leaving its seriousness. Educated at the Lycée Louis-le-Grand and the École Normale Supérieure (agrégation 1882), he taught philosophy in provincial lycées, spent a formative period in German universities, and in 1887 received at Bordeaux the first post in France with "social science" in its title. The four books that built the discipline followed: *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893), *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895), *Suicide* (1897), and — after his move to the Sorbonne (1902), where he held the chair until his death — *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912). Around the journal he founded, *L'Année Sociologique* (1898), he assembled the school that made sociology an institution, his nephew Marcel Mauss among them. A committed republican and Dreyfusard, he threw the discipline's weight behind the Third Republic's secular morality. The war destroyed the school's generation and then its founder: his son André died on the Salonika front in 1915, and Durkheim, broken, died 15 November 1917, aged 59.

## The programme

**Social facts, and the rules for studying them.** *The Rules* is the methodological manifesto: treat social facts as things; recognise them by exteriority and constraint (the currency you did not choose, the language you must speak to be heard, the obligations that outlive your consent); explain the social by the social. The polemical target was reduction — explaining institutions by individual psychology — which Durkheim held to be a category error: the association itself is productive, and what it produces has its own laws.

**Division of labour, and anomie.** The first book asks what holds modern societies together when shared belief thins. His answer: solidarity changes kind — from *mechanical* (likeness, common conscience, repressive law) to *organic* (difference, interdependence, restitutive law). The pathology of the transition he named **anomie**: normlessness, the state in which the regulating framework fails to keep pace with change — a concept that escaped the book and became a permanent tool.

**Suicide.** The demonstration piece: the most private act shown to have social causes. Stable, society-specific suicide rates — varying with religion, family, integration, regulation — are facts no psychology of individuals explains; Durkheim's typology (egoistic, altruistic, anomic, fatalistic) reads each kind off a deficit or excess of integration or regulation. Methodologically it is the discipline's founding empirical study; its statistics have been re-analysed and contested ever since, and its central claim — the social patterning of the personal — has survived every re-analysis.

**Religion, and the collective made visible.** *The Elementary Forms* completes the arc: religion as society worshipping itself — the sacred as the form in which the group's own force appears to its members. Collective representations (the shared categories in which a society thinks) and **collective effervescence** (the assembled group generating an energy no member brought) are the mechanisms; even the categories of thought — time, space, class, cause — Durkheim traces to social origins. It is the boldest statement of the sui generis claim: not just behaviour but the very frames of thought as products of association.

**The live disputes.** Methodological individualism has contested the programme from the start: social "things," the critics run (from Tarde in Durkheim's own day to rational-choice theory and analytical sociology since), are patterns of individual action, and explanation bottoms out in individuals — the social sciences' own version of the exclusion argument. Durkheim's defenders answer with his own bronze: relational organisation is real and does explanatory work no inventory of individuals replaces. The debate is the discipline's permanent fault line, not a settled episode; every generation restates both sides in its own vocabulary.

---

## Where Durkheim stops

The programme explains the social by the social, and its boundary is the join it declines to theorise: *how* individual association generates the sui generis level — the mechanism between interacting consciousnesses and the emergent fact — is asserted by analogy (the bronze, the compound) rather than supplied. Durkheim acknowledged the join's difficulty and worked it late (effervescence is his nearest approach to a mechanism); what he did not do is give the micro-to-macro account his own ontology presupposes, and the discipline he founded has been supplying candidates ever since. The constraint face of social facts also drew its permanent question — how the constrained ever change what constrains them — the structure-and-agency problem, named after him and left to his heirs.

## Key works

- *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893) — mechanical and organic solidarity; anomie introduced
- *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895) — social facts as things; the discipline's charter
- *Suicide: A Study in Sociology* (1897) — the founding empirical demonstration
- *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912) — the sacred, collective representations, effervescence
- "Individual and Collective Representations" (1898) — the sui generis claim argued directly
