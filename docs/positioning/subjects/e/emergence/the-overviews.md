# The Overviews of Emergence

A field's overviews show how it maps itself — what it treats as its centre, its history, and its edges. The emergence literature is vast; its overview genre is small enough to survey on one page. This page describes each overview's frame and coverage; the types themselves are catalogued on [the types page](/positioning/subjects/e/emergence/the-types/).

## The four general references

**The Stanford Encyclopedia entry, "Emergent Properties"** (maintained by Timothy O'Connor) is the analytic reference. Its organising frame is compatibility with physicalism: after the historical introduction (Mill and the British Emergentists), the entry analyses emergence as dependence-plus-autonomy, then divides the accounts into weak (physicalism-compatible — Bedau's incompressibility among them) and strong (closure-violating), closing with the contested phenomena — consciousness, cases from physics, and scientific pluralism — with quantum chemistry and emergent spacetime among its open questions. Its open questions are the field's: whether weak emergence is more than epistemic, whether strong emergence is coherent, and what the chemistry interface shows.

**The Internet Encyclopedia entry, "Emergence,"** is the historical arc: British Emergentism (Mill, Alexander, Morgan, Broad), the later emergentism that revived the vocabulary, and the standing objections. It works with the strong/weak and synchronic/diachronic sortings, addresses the divide between scientific usage (weak, complexity-flavoured) and philosophical usage (strong, mind-directed), and notes the quantum-mechanics reversal — the theory that undid classical emergentism returning, in later hands, as a candidate site of genuine emergence.

**Bedau and Humphreys' anthology, *Emergence: Contemporary Readings in Philosophy and Science*** (MIT, 2008), is the field's collection: twenty-four canonical papers across philosophy, physics, and artificial intelligence — Hempel and Oppenheim, Nagel, Searle, [Anderson](/positioning/persons/a/anderson/)'s "More is Different," Simon, Schelling, Dennett, Wolfram — held together by an editorial introduction organised around the tension between the *sparse* view (emergence as rare and exceptional) and the *radical* view (emergence as commonplace). Its reviewers noted one gap: the social-simulation and agent-based literature is absent.

**Humphreys' handbook chapter, "Emergence"** (Oxford Handbook of Philosophy of Science, 2016), is the methodological survey. Its opening verdicts frame the genre: a unified theory or concise definition of emergence "is not a feasible project at the current time," and — unlike causation — the field has no agreed core set of examples against which accounts can be tested; assembling one, Humphreys suggests, would be a useful project. He sorts the senses into ontological, epistemological, inferential, and conceptual emergence, diagnoses the literature as two camps with a widening gap — one open to emergence within the sciences, one driven by metaphysics of mind, whose narrowing of the question he calls crippling — and dismantles the assumed opposition between emergence and reduction in both directions.

## The monograph surveys

**Clayton and Davies (eds.), *The Re-Emergence of Emergence*** (Oxford, 2006) marked the revival: scientists and philosophers surveying emergence from physics to consciousness, with the theological adjacency its title signals. **Gillett's *Reduction and Emergence in Science and Philosophy*** (Cambridge, 2016) is the systematic metaphysics-side treatment, building a framework for both notions across the sciences. **Humphreys' *Emergence: A Philosophical Account*** (Oxford, 2016) is the monograph companion to his chapter, developing the diachronic and transformational accounts. **[Wilson](/positioning/persons/w/wilson-jm/)'s *Metaphysical Emergence*** (Oxford, 2021) functions as a survey through its central claim — that weak and strong metaphysical emergence, powers-defined, exhaust the coherent options — and reads the field's accounts as versions of its two schemas.

## The register-specific surveys

**Palacios' *Emergence and Reduction in Physics*** (Cambridge Elements, 2022) surveys the physics debate — phase transitions and limits included. **Sawyer's *Social Emergence*** (Cambridge, 2005) is the social sciences' survey, mapping the philosophical taxonomy onto sociology's own history from [Durkheim](/positioning/persons/d/durkheim/) forward. **Taylor's "An Explication of Emergence"** (*Philosophical Studies*, 2015) is the article-length analytic explication. The quantitative wing has its own recent survey — "Emergence and Causality in Complex Systems" (*Entropy*, 2024) — covering causal emergence and its measures. The engineering taxonomies (Fromm; De Wolf and Holvoet) are surveys in miniature and appear as rows on [the types page](/positioning/subjects/e/emergence/the-types/).

## The shape of the genre

The general overviews are philosophy's — organised by physicalism, by the British lineage, or by the analytic disputes — with the sciences appearing as cases at their edges, while the science registers are surveyed register by register: physics, sociology, and the quantitative wing each on its own. Humphreys' two-camps diagnosis, made from inside the genre, describes the division — one camp open to emergence within the sciences, one driven by the metaphysics of mind — and calls the second camp's narrowing of the question crippling to an understanding of emergence in general.
